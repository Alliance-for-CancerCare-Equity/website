# ACCE: Making Cancer Care Equitable

*Written by Seyitan Moritiwon*

A cancer diagnosis can change everything in an instant.

For patients and their families, the fear of the disease itself is often accompanied by another question: How will we afford the care we need?

Cancer treatment can involve chemotherapy, radiotherapy, diagnostic scans, medications and repeated hospital visits, with average costs reaching nearly [CAD 33,000 in Canada](https://cancer.ca/en/get-involved/advocacy/cost-of-cancer). For families already facing financial hardship, these costs can become overwhelming. And when treatment becomes unaffordable, patients may be forced to delay care, miss appointments or make impossible choices between their health and their family's basic needs.

This is where equitable cancer care matters.

At the Alliance for CancerCare Equity (ACCE), support means meeting patients where they are and helping remove the financial barriers standing between them and the care they need. Behind that mission are real people whose lives have been changed because someone chose to help.

[Rukmini Adjetey's story](/patients-stories/rukmini-adjetey) is one of them.

In 2024, Adjetey was diagnosed with cancer in Ghana. This caused fear and worry about the future. Her life may quickly be filled with hospital visits, medical tests, and hard conversations about survival. The cost of cancer care was more than her family could afford.

As a result, she was connected to ACCE, whose donors helped cover the full cost of her cancer treatment. Rukmini no longer had to worry about how to pay for care. She could focus on healing her body, her mind, and her spirit.

Her experience reflects a central belief behind ACCE's work: access to quality cancer care should not depend on whether a person can afford it.

For [Yaw Emmanuel](/patients-stories/emmanuel-grusi), the consequences of cancer extended beyond his health.

In 2022, Emmanuel, a 39-year-old husband and father of two children was diagnosed with bladder cancer. As his health declined, so did his financial stability. Yaw was forced to close his shop, which had been his family's main source of income. Due to financial difficulties, he stopped attending his clinic appointments altogether.

A doctor at the oncology clinic in Kumasi, Ghana connected him with ACCE for help. And shortly after, he was able to return to the hospital and resume his treatment. The organization stepped in to support his chemotherapy and has continued to stand by him throughout all his treatment.

These are just a couple of the testimonials from patients who have received help from ACCE.

## More than financial assistance

Adjetey and Emmanuel's experiences demonstrate why cancer equity requires more than simply telling patients to seek treatment.

ACCE's mission is to promote equity in cancer care from prevention through survivorship, providing comprehensive support to patients and families facing the challenges of this life-threatening illness.

A cancer diagnosis does not exist in isolation from a person's financial circumstances. A patient may know they need chemotherapy but have no way to pay for it. They may be determined to continue treatment but be unable to afford transportation to the hospital. A family may understand the care a loved one needs but lack the resources to make that care possible.

These barriers can turn a treatable illness into an even greater crisis.

ACCE works to help close that gap.

Through financial assistance and patient-centred support, ACCE helps patients navigate some of the challenges that can stand between a cancer diagnosis and meaningful treatment. The goal is not simply to provide funding. It is to empower people to continue their care, protect their dignity and give them a better chance to focus on recovery.

## A Community of Hope

Behind every ACCE patient is a network of people who make care possible, including donors, healthcare professionals, families and volunteers, helping connect these pieces so that patients are not left to navigate cancer alone.

Every contribution has the potential to become something much bigger than a donation.

Rukmini and Yaw's stories show what can happen when support reaches people at the moment they need it most.

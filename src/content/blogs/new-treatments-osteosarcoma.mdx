# New Treatments on the Horizon for Osteosarcoma

*Written by Seyitan Moritiwon*

When most people think of cancer, they rarely think of it starting in the bones. Yet each year, hundreds of children, teenagers, and young adults face osteosarcoma, a rare and aggressive bone cancer that often appears during periods of rapid growth.

Osteosarcoma is a type of sarcoma, a group of cancers that develop in connective tissues such as bones, muscles, fat, tendons, cartilage, blood vessels, nerves, and fibrous tissue. In the United States, about [1,000 new osteosarcoma cases](https://osinst.org/blog/the-osteosarcoma-institute-faqs/) are diagnosed each year, highlighting the need for continued research and new treatment approaches.

## What are the treatment options for osteosarcoma?

Treating osteosarcoma often requires a combination of therapies. Currently, the standard approach includes chemotherapy and surgery, which work together to control the disease and remove the tumor.

Chemotherapy is typically given for several months before surgery to help shrink the tumor and is continued afterward to target any remaining cancer cells. Depending on the tumor's location and other factors, surgery may involve limb-preservation procedures that allow patients to keep their affected limb. Physical rehabilitation usually begins during recovery to help patients regain strength and mobility.

## New research

While current treatments have improved survival for many osteosarcoma patients, options remain limited, especially for those with advanced or metastatic disease. Researchers are now exploring new approaches that harness the power of the immune system to fight this rare cancer.

A [new study](https://case.edu/news/researchers-develop-promising-new-therapy-most-common-form-bone-cancer-children-and-young-adults) by [Case Western Reserve University](https://case.edu/) and [University Hospitals](https://www.uhhospitals.org/) has found that a specially engineered immune-cell treatment, called OSM CAR-T, successfully attacks osteosarcoma tumours in mice.

CAR-T (Chimaeric Antigen Receptor T-cell) therapy is an advanced treatment that reprograms a patient's immune cells to recognise and destroy cancer cells.

"For the past 40 years, conventional therapies for osteosarcoma, which consist of chemotherapy and surgery, have not changed much," said [Reshmi Parameswaran](https://case.edu/cancer/members/member-directory/reshmi-parameswaran), the lead researcher of the study, which was published in [BMC Medicine](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12916-026-04729-8).

"Our new approach offers the possibility of a targeted treatment that harnesses the body's own immune system to fight the cancer—potentially offering better outcomes with fewer side effects than traditional chemotherapy," Parameswaran added.

Although CAR-T therapy has helped with treatment for blood cancers such as leukaemia and lymphoma, it has proven less effective against solid tumours such as osteosarcoma.

As the [National Cancer Institute (NCI)](https://www.cancer.gov/) noted, cancer cells in solid tumors are more complex and display different markers on their surfaces, which makes it difficult for CAR-T cells to find and attack all the cancer cells effectively.

The study solved this problem by creating a CAR-T cell that targets receptors of a protein called Oncostatin M (OSM) that appears on the surface of osteosarcoma cells. It shows a new approach to treat osteosarcoma, allowing the engineered immune cells to identify multiple receptors on cancer cells simultaneously.

The team anticipates the new treatment will be tested in clinical trials within two years. If successful, it could be offered as a new option, reducing the need for surgery and providing hope for patients with metastatic disease.

## Virtual reality

Beyond new therapies, emerging technologies such as virtual reality (VR) and artificial intelligence (AI) are opening new possibilities for improving osteosarcoma treatment, particularly in surgical planning.

[Dr. Nathan Mesko](https://providers.clevelandclinic.org/provider/nathan-mesko/4270826), vice chair of global clinical operations for the Department of Orthopaedic Surgery and center director for musculoskeletal oncology, in a recent episode of Cleveland Clinic's [Cancer Advances podcast](https://www.buzzsprout.com/2241774/episodes/18742908-innovations-transforming-osteosarcoma-outcomes), discusses some of the challenges associated with osteosarcomas.

He believes that VR can be used to overlay anatomic structures to determine the location of a tumor or edema field, which can improve surgical planning. Combined with AI, these tools could provide more detailed information to support surgical decisions.

"The problem right now is that it's all after the fact," he says. "I have to cut the tumor out, and then we look at the necrosis in everything. It doesn't help me plan or execute my surgery."

But if a Positron emission tomography (PET) scan or a Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) could be used and analyzed by virtual augmentation or AI, surgeons could drastically improve their decision-making.

"What if we could actually say with a high degree of certainty, 'Based on this MRI, you have "X" amount of necrosis.' It really lets me go in with a much more confident understanding of the biology that I'm dealing with, going into the operating room…I think the sky is the limit when it comes to what technology can enable us to do," Dr. Mesko said.

# The Sensory Survival Kit: Anchoring Yourself Through Cancer

*Written by Jaspreet Kour*

A cancer diagnosis doesn't just affect the body. It affects the mind, too. Research shows that nearly [one in three people with cancer](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2014/10/141006174142.htm) experience a clinically meaningful level of mental or emotional distress, including anxiety and depression, at some point in their journey. Some studies show that anxiety affects [up to 44% of people with cancer](https://www.sarh.org/services/cancer-center/learn-about-cancer/anxiety-in-patients-with-cancer). Some days bring hope. Other days bring fear, fatigue, or a feeling of being completely out of control. Both are normal, and neither one needs to be faced alone.

On the hardest days, it can feel impossible to "calm down" or "think positive." No one expects you to. Healing isn't about forcing your emotions away, it's about finding small, gentle ways to bring yourself back to the present moment, one breath and one sense at a time. This is where a sensory survival kit can help.

## What Is a Sensory Survival Kit?

A sensory survival kit uses your five senses (touch, smell, sound, taste, and sight) to gently anchor you when anxiety, fatigue, or stress feel overwhelming. The idea comes from a well-studied practice called [grounding](https://polkcountybehavioralhealthresources.org/blog/coping-by-using-the-five-sense/), a form of mindfulness that helps the brain shift attention away from distressing thoughts and back to the present moment.

Grounding doesn't erase what you're going through, and it isn't a substitute for medical or mental health support. What it does is give your mind and body a place to rest, even if only for a few minutes at a time, a small pause in a day that can otherwise feel like it's moving too fast, or standing painfully still.

## Why This Actually Works

It's not just a feel-good idea, there's real science behind it. Grounding techniques work by interrupting the cycle of anxious or distressing thoughts and re-engaging the parts of the brain connected to your physical senses, which helps calm the nervous system. Studies on cancer patients specifically have shown that mind-body practices like these can meaningfully reduce anxiety, stress, and even the perception of pain during treatment.

Music is one of the most researched examples. A [meta-analysis of 15 randomized controlled trials](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9853974/) involving more than 1,300 cancer patients found that listening to music produced a moderate but consistent reduction in cancer-related anxiety compared to standard care alone. Other trials have found that even a [single group music therapy session during chemotherapy](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1658503/full) led to a measurable drop in anxiety. [Art therapy has shown similar promise](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1155/2020/7390321), particularly for processing difficult emotions that are hard to put into words.

In short: engaging your senses isn't a distraction from healing. For many patients, it's part of it.

## Building Your Kit, One Sense at a Time

You don't need anything expensive or complicated to build a sensory survival kit. A shoebox, a small bag, or even a note on your phone with a list of go-to comforts can work. Here's where to start.

**Touch** — Keep something soft nearby, such as a warm blanket, a smooth stone, a stress ball, a knitted scarf, or even a pet's fur. Focusing on texture and temperature can help pull your mind out of a spiral of worry and back into your body. Some patients also find comfort in a gentle hand or foot massage, which may help ease tension and physical discomfort. Just check with your healthcare team first, especially during active treatment.

**Smell** — Scent is powerfully linked to memory and emotion. A favourite lotion, a sachet of lavender, a scented candle (unlit if you're in a hospital setting), or a cup of tea can offer a moment of calm. If you're going through chemotherapy, scent sensitivity can change quickly, so it's worth checking with your care team about which smells are safe and tolerable for you right now.

**Sound** — A favourite playlist, a calming podcast, a voice memo from a loved one, or even the sound of rain can help quiet a racing mind. As the research above shows, music in particular has been studied extensively in oncology settings and consistently shows benefits for anxiety. Ask your care team if a music therapy program is available where you're being treated. Many cancer centres now offer one.

**Taste** — Keep a small treat close by, for example, mints, ginger candy, herbal tea, or a favourite snack. Slowly noticing the taste and texture, rather than eating on autopilot, can be a simple way to stay present, especially during moments of anxiety or nausea.

**Sight** — Surround yourself with something that brings comfort: a photo of loved ones, a plant, a piece of art, or a view of the outdoors. If you're able, even a short walk outside can help reset your mind. Some patients also find it grounding to keep a small "vision" item nearby. Something as simple as a postcard from a place they love or hope to visit again.

## A Simple Way to Start Right Now

If you're not sure where to begin, try the [5-4-3-2-1 technique](https://psychcentral.com/anxiety/using-the-five-senses-for-anxiety-relief#5-4-3-2-1-technique): name 5 things you can see, 4 things you can touch, 3 things you can hear, 2 things you can smell, and 1 thing you can taste. It sounds almost too simple, but small studies have shown this kind of sensory grounding can meaningfully reduce anxiety in the moment. In one study, it lowered the number of participants reporting high anxiety from 23 percent to just 4 percent.

![Five Senses grounding technique: 5 things you can see, 4 you can feel, 3 you can hear, 2 you can smell, 1 you can taste](/images/five_senses_grounding.png)

You can do this anywhere: in a waiting room, during a treatment session, or lying awake at 2 a.m. when your thoughts won't slow down. There's no wrong way to do it.

## A Note for Caregivers, Too

If you're supporting someone through cancer, this applies to you as well. Caregiving can be emotionally demanding, and it's easy to put your own needs last. A sensory survival kit isn't only for patients. Caregivers can use the same tools to manage their own stress, even in small five-minute pockets between appointments, errands, and care duties. Looking after your own well-being isn't selfish. It's what allows you to keep showing up for the person you love.

## You Don't Have to Carry This Alone

There is no single "right" way to cope. What matters is having a few small tools ready for the days that feel heavier than others, and knowing that reaching for them isn't a sign of weakness. It's a sign of taking care of yourself.

Be patient with yourself. Some days a sensory kit will help. Other days, what you need is simply someone to talk to; a friend, a counsellor, a support group, or your healthcare team. All of it counts as coping. All of it counts as healing.
